Chronicle & Origin

Who, when, and by whom?

Delhi is ancient, but New Delhi was invented on the drawing board! It was officially founded in 1911 by the British colonial rulers under King George V, designed by star architects Edwin Lutyens and Herbert Baker. They wanted a monumental metropolis to replace Calcutta as the capital. It was solemnly inaugurated in 1931.

Historical Power

The 7 Cities Before Modernity

Who used to live here? Delhi was the most coveted powerhouse in Asia! Over the centuries, seven great empires clashed here. Famous Mughal emperors like Shah Jahan (the builder of the Taj Mahal) ruled their vast empire from this very place. Before them, brutal sultans and proud Rajputs held power. Every era left behind ruins that now stand right in the middle of modern traffic.

The Evolution

What happened over the years?

From a royal Mughal seat to a colonial fortress, and finally to the heart of independent India. Delhi was destroyed, plundered (among others by the Persian ruler Nadir Shah), and rebuilt time and again like a phoenix from the ashes. Today, history collides: centuries-old mosques stand in the narrow alleys of Old Delhi, while wide, European-style grand avenues define New Delhi.

Power of Today

Who lives here today?

Today, the Delhi metropolitan area is one of the largest conurbations on Earth, with over 35 million inhabitants! Here, diplomats, IT billionaires, and politicians live side by side with millions of migrant workers and rickshaw drivers. As the political capital, Delhi is the undisputed epicenter of Indian democracy and economic power.

Top Attractions in the Metropolis

These monumental landmarks are absolute must-sees on your journey.

1. The India Gate

Right in the heart of the city stands the majestic India Gate, a 42-meter-high triumphal arch. It was solemnly inaugurated in 1931 in the colonial Lutyens style as a national memorial.

The monument honors the tens of thousands of Indian soldiers who fell in the First World War. Beneath the arch burns the eternal flame, honoring the country’s immortal soldiers.
Today, families, street vendors, and travelers gather here in the evenings amidst lush green lawns.

2. The Qutb Minar

This gigantic, 73-meter-high victory monument instantly captivates every visitor. It was built in the late twelfth century from red sandstone and fine marble.

The minaret is recognized worldwide as one of the earliest and most impressive examples of Indo-Islamic architecture. Intricate, delicate Quranic verses and ornamental stonemasonry are carved into its five stories.
The UNESCO World Heritage site is nestled within a park filled with fascinating ancient ruins.

3. Humayun's Tomb

This splendid tomb complex from 1572 is an architectural jewel of the Mughal era. It was commissioned by the widow of the Mughal Emperor Humayun in a distinctive Persian style.
Historically, this massive structure served as the direct visual blueprint for the later Taj Mahal. The symmetrical facade is completely enclosed by elaborately designed, geometric gardens.
Travelers particularly appreciate the spiritual tranquility here, far away from the roaring noise of the big city.

4. The Lotus Temple

This modern masterpiece of architecture fascinates with its shape of a white lotus flower. The structure consists of twenty-seven massive petals made of the finest Greek Pentelic marble.
As a Bahá'í House of Worship, this sacred site is completely open to all religions equally.
The minimalist interior deliberately lacks any statues of gods, pictures, or altars. Nine clear, deep-blue water pools surround the building, symbolizing spiritual purity.

The Ultimate Culture Shock: One Step, Two Worlds

Nowhere else on Earth do centuries and realities of life clash as brutally and demandingly as at the invisible border between Old and New.

Old Delhi: The Labyrinth of the Senses

You step out of the Chandni Chowk metro station and are instantly hit by a wall of heat, noise, and smells. The alleys are so narrow that the sky above you shrinks to a slender strip, crisscrossed by a treacherous thicket of thousands of black power cables. Rickshaw drivers shout, bicycle bells ring every second, and the sweet scent of deep-fried jalebis mingles with the pungent smell of ground chili, burning incense, and the exhaust fumes of vintage Vespas.

Multitudes of people push you relentlessly forward, past tiny wooden stalls where silver, silk sarees, or heavy sacks of spices have been piled up for generations. The ground is uneven, puddles reflect the harsh light of bare lightbulbs, and the walls of the centuries-old haveli houses crumble with pride and age. It is claustrophobic, it is deafening, it is completely unfiltered, pulsating life — a sensory overload that takes your breath away.

New Delhi: Imperial Geometry

Just a few kilometers south, reality cuts through the city like a scalpel, and suddenly the world opens up. You stand on Rajpath, New Delhi's gigantic ceremonial avenue, and the noise fades almost instantly into a distant background hum. Your gaze sweeps across perfectly manicured, deep-green lawns, miles of tree-lined avenues, and monumental government buildings made of red and yellow sandstone. Where Old Delhi was defined by cramped confinement, an almost intimidating colonial vastness dominates here, calculated exactly so by the British rulers.

Splendid bungalows behind high walls, guarded by uniformed security forces, line the clean, shaded streets of the Lutyens zone. Expensive SUVs glide silently over the asphalt, while in the background, the India Gate stands proudly against the hazy sky. It is the world of diplomats, power, and money. This stark transition feels like time travel: within twenty minutes, you have traveled from the deep, medieval heart of the subcontinent into the sterile, geometric modernity of a global superpower.
