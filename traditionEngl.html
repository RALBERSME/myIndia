Traditional & Religious Holidays

India's calendar is characterized by an immense diversity. Since Indian culture is deeply rooted in spirituality, most festivals are based on mythological epics and lunar calendars.

Diwali (Deepavali) – The Festival of Lights

Celebrated by: Hindus, Jains, Sikhs | Period: October/November




Origins & Significance: Diwali celebrates the return of Lord Rama from his 14-year exile and his victory over the demon king Ravana. It symbolizes the triumph of light over darkness and good over evil. Historically, it also marked the end of the harvest season and the beginning of a new business year.

How it is celebrated: Houses are meticulously cleaned and decorated with small oil lamps (diyas) and colorful sand art (rangolis). Families come together, exchange sweets (mithai), light fireworks, and pray to Goddess Lakshmi for prosperity.

Changing Significance: In the past, spiritual reflection, harvest thanksgiving, and the ritual lighting of clay lamps were central. Today, Diwali has additionally transformed into a massive commercial and shopping event, comparable to Christmas in the West.

Holi – The Festival of Colors

Celebrated by: Predominantly Hindus (cross-societal) | Period: March




Origins & Significance: It is based, among other things, on the burning of the demoness Holika (the victory of devotion over evil) and celebrates the carefree love between Lord Krishna and Radha. At the same time, it marks the arrival of spring.

How it is celebrated: People throw colored powder (gulal) and tinted water at one another. Rigid caste barriers, age gaps, and social status boundaries are completely suspended for this single day—everyone is equal.

Changing Significance: Historically, only natural medicinal herbs and flower extracts (such as neem or turmeric) were used as colors, which also served to prevent springtime illnesses. Today, synthetic colors often dominate, and the festival has inspired secular spin-offs worldwide (like Color Runs).

Eid ul-Fitr & Eid al-Adha

Celebrated by: The Muslim minority (approx. 200 million Indians) | Period: Varies




Origins & Significance: Eid ul-Fitr marks the end of the fasting month of Ramadan. Eid al-Adha is the Festival of Sacrifice, commemorating Abraham's willingness to sacrifice his son. Both celebrations are integral parts of pan-Indian culture.

How it is celebrated: Following congregational prayers at major mosques, families and friends feast opulently. A traditional Indian dish for this occasion is Sheer Khurma (a sweet milk pudding with dates and vermicelli). It is customary to invite non-Muslim neighbors as well.

Changing Significance: In both the past and present, these festivals serve as structural pillars of social welfare (Zakat/almsgiving) and, despite political tensions, frequently strengthen interfaith harmony locally (Ganga-Jamuni Tehzeeb).

Customs, Traditions & Social Ethics

Indian society is collectivist in nature. The well-being and reputation of the family, as well as respect for elders, take precedence over individual desires.

Accepted & Praiseworthy

  • Respect for Age: Elderly people are treated with deep reverence. A traditional custom is Charan Sparsh — touching the feet of grandparents, parents, or teachers to seek their blessings.
  • Hospitality (Atithi Devo Bhava): The guest is quite literally treated like a god. It is considered impolite to vehemently refuse offered food or tea (chai).
  • Everyday Religious Tolerance: Accepting and mutually congratulating others on the festivals of different faith communities is regarded as ethically commendable. // Taboo & Unacceptable

Taboo & Unacceptable

  • The Impure Left Hand: Historically, the left hand is considered impure (associated with personal hygiene). It is an absolute taboo to offer or receive food, money, or gifts with the left hand.
  • Shoes inside Houses/Temples: Wearing outdoor shoes inside living rooms or religious sites is seen as a gross sign of disrespect and contamination.
  • Public Displays of Affection: Exchanging intimacies (kissing, intense hugging) in public is ethically considered unseemly and is socially frowned upon.

Manners & Everyday Etiquette

The Right Greeting+

The traditional"Namaste" (or "Namaskar"), where the palms are brought together in front of the chest along with a slight bow, is always correct. It expresses deep respect without needing to physically touch the other person. Shaking hands is common among men in business contexts, but should be approached with more distance between men and women — here, it is best to wait and see if the woman offers her hand first.

Table Manners & Eating with Fingers +

Traditional Indian food (like roti or rice with curry) is often eaten with the right hand. Only the fingertips are used to bring the food to the mouth; the palm should remain clean. If cutlery is provided, it can, of course, be used. It is considered polite to wash your hands thoroughly before and after eating.

Dress Code +

India is conservative when it comes to clothing. Shoulders and knees should always be covered for both women and men. Excessive revealing clothing in everyday life (e.g., short shorts or low-cut tops) attracts unwanted attention and is considered impolite. When entering temples, mosques, or Gurudwaras (Sikh temples), women (and sometimes men as well) must also cover their heads with a scarf.

Communication & The Indian Head Wobble +

Criticism is rarely expressed directly in India to avoid causing the other person to "lose face." A direct "no" is often paraphrased. Famous is the Indian head wobble (a sideways tilting of the head). It is not a clear "yes" or "no," but usually means "OK," "I understand," or signals friendly attentiveness during a conversation.
